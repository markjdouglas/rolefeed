Product brief

Decision · 21 September 2026

Stop. The hypothesis was tested and is not supported. In its current form RoleFeed is not an effective way to find a role, and it is not superior to LinkedIn on any dimension that decides the outcome.

It works as engineering and fails as a product. Seven roles cleared the filter from 211 tracked employers, and every one of the seven was already visible on LinkedIn. Development as a job-search tool stops here. Two uses survive, both narrow, and they are set out below.

01 Problem

Finding a senior operations role in transport and logistics means checking five sites daily, each returning the same padded results, with filters too blunt to express the actual search: senior operations, transport and mobility, UK or a workable time zone, above a seniority floor that moves with company size.

The sharper problem was timing. The working belief was that applications land better inside the first 48 hours, before a posting accumulates several hundred applicants, and that most tools cannot tell you a posting's age with any confidence — so they cannot help you act on it.

Who it was for

One user. No second user was ever contemplated, which is worth stating because it removes every network effect from the design space and, as it turns out, decides the outcome.

02 Goals and non-goals

Goals

  • Surface roles worth applying for that would not otherwise have been seen.
  • Know a posting's true age, so the 48-hour window can be acted on.
  • Learn how the parts fit together — APIs, git, CI, deployment — by building and shipping something real.

Non-goals

  • Scraping anything, or any paid aggregator.
  • Automated applications. It surfaces; applying stays manual.
  • A second user, an account system, or anything resembling a marketplace.
  • A framework or a build step. One static file, deployed as assets.

04 Hypotheses, against the four product risks

Marty Cagan's framing, because it separates "can we build it" from "should we" — a distinction this project needed and did not apply.

FeasibilityCan it be built with the time, skills and technology available?Passed
ViabilityDoes it work for the business — cost, effort, upkeep?Passed
UsabilityCan the user work out how to use it?Passed
ValueWill anyone choose it over what they already have?Failed

Cagan's instruction is to test value and viability risk first. I tested feasibility first, because building six applicant-tracking-system adapters was the interesting problem. I established that I could build it before asking whether it was worth building. That is the standard builder's error, it cost about five hours here, and at organisational scale it is how a quarter disappears.

05 Method

Employers publish vacancies through applicant tracking systems, and several of those expose an open, keyless JSON endpoint that the employer's own careers page consumes. A curated employer list is polled every two hours; results are filtered on geography and seniority, merged with the previous run so first-seen dates survive, and written as one static JSON file that a static page renders.

The constraint that shaped everything

No applicant tracking system offers search. Every endpoint is scoped to one employer, so there is no query to write and nothing to crawl — you can only ask a named employer what it currently has open. The code is commodity; the curated list is the product, and coverage is bounded by who is on it.

Why, and it is structural

Splitting the 62 reachable employers by the kind of system they were reached through gives a monotonic gradient: 31% of enterprise employers came via Greenhouse, Lever or Ashby, against 80% of large, 89% of mid, 89% of scaleup and 100% of startup. Those three are the tooling of venture-funded technology companies. Incumbents buy an HR suite — SuccessFactors, Taleo, iCIMS — that publishes nothing.

So the method's reach is inversely correlated with the employers it is aimed at. Lengthening the list cannot fix that: going from 169 employers to 211 moved reachability seven points and left the shape untouched, because a longer list adds reachable and unreachable employers in the same proportion.

07 Benchmark: why LinkedIn wins

This was never benchmarked before building, which is the second process error. LinkedIn had 1.3 billion registered members as of March 2026, 1.4 billion monthly visits, and $17.8 billion of revenue in 2025 — and the material fact is where the revenue comes from: selling access to member data to recruiters. LinkedIn is not a job board. It is a candidate graph sold to the demand side. Five consequences, each fatal on its own.

  • Coverage runs the wrong way. RoleFeed reaches 62 of 211 employers it chose, bounded by which HR system each bought years ago. Employers push to LinkedIn because it is a distribution channel they pay for. One ceiling is structural; the other does not exist.
  • It is two-sided and this is not. For a director-level role most of the value is inbound — being found by a recruiter searching the graph. RoleFeed is a read-only window. It cannot make its user visible to anyone.
  • The roles most wanted are disproportionately unadvertised. Senior operations posts are filled through recruiters and referrals, which is a social-graph problem. LinkedIn is a social graph. RoleFeed has none and cannot acquire one.
  • Freshness was the differentiator and it is not one. The bet was a two-hour collection cycle. LinkedIn timestamps postings, sorts by recency and offers one-click apply. Two hours of latency plus a manual application is slower end to end than the thing being beaten.
  • Matching. Behavioural data across 1.3 billion profiles against regular expressions tuned by hand — which have produced three classification defects in two days.

What RoleFeed does do better, stated narrowly

Determinism and auditability. Every role shows which rule matched it, there is no ranking algorithm, no promoted posts and no engagement optimisation, and the coverage page states exactly which employers were not considered. LinkedIn cannot tell you what it chose not to show you. That is a genuine property and it is worth something in a compliance or sourcing context. It does not survive a threefold coverage gap and the absence of a demand side.

08 Decision and what survives

Persevere, pivot or stop: stop, as a job-search tool. The search itself runs through recruiters and LinkedIn, which is where the roles and the people who fill them actually are. Two things survive.

  • A coverage instrument. The one thing RoleFeed does that nothing else does is name the 149 employers whose hiring is invisible to it. That is a genuinely useful list and no job board will ever produce it, because no job board has a reason to tell you what it cannot see.
  • A demonstration of method. A working pipeline, a measured funnel, a falsified hypothesis and a documented stop decision, built in about five hours at no cost.

The pivot worth testing, if any

Invert it. Stop hunting roles and use the unreachable list to find employers whose hiring is invisible, then approach them directly — a weakness turned into a targeting list, on the argument that an employer no aggregator can see is an employer with less applicant competition. That is a different product with a different hypothesis, and it should be tested on a spreadsheet before anything is built.

09 What I would do differently

  • Test value first, with no code. An afternoon checking twenty employers' careers pages by hand would have produced the same seven roles and the same conclusion, without six adapters.
  • Benchmark before building. "What does LinkedIn already give me here?" is a five-minute question that would have reframed the whole project, and I asked it after the build rather than before.
  • Write the success metric before the first line of code. "Five roles worth applying for" was set honestly but retrospectively, and a metric defined after the fact cannot kill anything.
  • Separate the learning goal from the utility goal. Both were real. Conflating them let a failing product feel like a succeeding one, because the learning was going extremely well the whole time. That is the most transferable mistake here, and the easiest one to repeat with a team.

10 Open questions

  • Would probing eight further systems — Workable, Personio, Pinpoint, Applied, Eploy, Jobtrain, Tribepad, Hireserve — clear 40% enterprise reachability? A day's work, and the only remaining test of the original hypothesis.
  • Does the unreachable list have value on its own as a targeting list?
  • Is auditability worth anything to anyone other than its author?
A tool that answered its own question in the negative, which is the cheapest useful outcome available. Changes are in the version history; what cannot be seen is on the coverage page.
