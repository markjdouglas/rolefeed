The problem

Why this exists

Job boards optimise for the board, not the applicant. Aggregators are slow, duplicated and padded with roles that are already weeks old, and the filters they offer are too blunt to express a specific search: senior operations, transport and mobility, UK or a workable time zone, and above a seniority floor that shifts with company size. The result is a daily scan of five sites that surfaces the same thirty irrelevant roles.

Freshness is the part that matters most. The working assumption is that applications land better inside the first 48 hours, before a posting has accumulated several hundred applicants. Most tools cannot tell you a posting's age with any confidence, so they cannot help you act on it.

The original hypothesis

Employers publish their vacancies through applicant tracking systems, and most of those systems expose an open, keyless JSON endpoint that the employer's own careers page consumes. If that is true at scale, a curated list of employers can be polled directly — no scraping, no aggregator, no paid API — and the result is fresher and cleaner than anything downstream of it.

Stated that way it is one claim doing five jobs, which is why it took real data to work out which part was wrong. Separated out:

Employers expose open, keyless endpointsPartly True of one class of employer, false of another
A curated employer list is enough for coverageNo Coverage is bounded by system, not by list length
Direct polling beats aggregators on freshnessYes Two-hour cycle, genuine publication dates
Filtering can isolate relevant roles preciselyUntested The tiered rule has not completed a run
The resulting flow is large enough to be usefulNot yet Four matches end to end

The second line is the one that failed, and it failed for a reason the original framing could not see. The evidence below is what exposed it.

The proposed mechanism was that applicant tracking system adoption correlates with company type — that Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby are the tooling of venture-funded technology companies, while incumbents buy an HR suite that publishes nothing. Confirmed, and the gradient is monotonic. 31%, 80%, 89%, 89%, 100% — the larger the employer, the less likely it is reachable through the three endpoints the project was originally built on. Every enterprise employer recovered so far came through SmartRecruiters or Teamtailor: GXO, Maersk, Geodis, Europcar, Sixt, Avis, Mobico, Delivery Hero, Grab. None came through Greenhouse.

So the method's reach is inversely correlated with the target market. That is a structural property of the approach, not a gap in the employer list, and lengthening the list cannot fix it — going from 169 to 211 employers moved reachability by seven points and left the shape of the problem untouched.

The second candidate cause is that the title rules are too tight. The four matches above were produced by a single accept-or-reject threshold; the three-tier classifier and the expanded employer list have not yet completed a collection, so that cause is untested rather than ruled out. The third is that the market is genuinely thin — there may simply be very few senior transport operations roles open in the UK this fortnight. Nothing here distinguishes the second from the third, and the next run distinguishes only the second.

Honest reading: the approach is proven as an engineering method and unproven as a sourcing strategy. It reliably retrieves fresh, well-dated, structured postings at zero cost. Whether it can retrieve the right ones depends on closing a coverage gap that sits precisely where the approach is weakest.

The revised hypothesis

Reverse-engineered from the evidence above

The original design treated the employer as the unit of coverage, so the obvious lever was a longer list. The data says the unit of coverage is not the employer at all — it is the publishing surface the employer happens to sit behind. Two employers of identical size and sector are reachable or unreachable purely on which HR software they bought, a decision made years ago by someone who was not thinking about job seekers. Lengthening the list adds employers in the same proportion of reachable to unreachable, which is why 169 employers and 211 employers produced almost the same number of matches.

So the claim worth testing next is not about employers:

Any employer that publishes its vacancies in a machine-readable form on the open web can be collected. The applicant tracking system API is one such surface, and it is the smallest one.

That reframing changes what counts as progress. Under the original hypothesis the enterprise bucket was simply lost, because SuccessFactors, Taleo and iCIMS have no API and there is nothing more to say. Under the revised one they are only lost through that surface, and a second surface exists: a careers page carrying a schema.org JobPosting in its markup is machine-readable by any reasonable definition, is published deliberately so that search engines can read it, and is completely independent of which system generated it.

Three things follow, in order of how much they would move the number.

  • Find the next Teamtailor first. This is a correction: the obvious response to a coverage gap in the enterprise bucket was to go after careers-page structured data, and the numbers say otherwise. Teamtailor alone took enterprise reachability from 15% to 22% in one afternoon, because it is what several logistics incumbents actually use — GXO, Maersk, Geodis, Europcar. Adding one adapter is a day's work with a known method and a proven precedent. So the next move is to probe the remaining candidate systems, prioritised by UK and enterprise market share rather than by familiarity: Workable, Personio, Pinpoint, Applied, Eploy, Jobtrain, Tribepad, Hireserve. Probe them; do not assume, since assuming is precisely how Teamtailor was missed for a week.
  • Then structured-data collection, for the residue. Some employers will have no open endpoint under any system — SuccessFactors, Taleo and iCIMS look genuinely closed. For those, a careers page carrying a schema.org JobPosting is machine-readable by any reasonable definition, published deliberately so that search engines can read it, and independent of whichever system generated it. That is legitimate in a way parsing a page's layout is not. It stays second because it is more work than an adapter and helps fewer employers than the adapters would.
  • Where no machine-readable surface exists, the employer is genuinely out of scope and should be recorded as such. British Airways publishes no structured data and disallows its search path to crawlers; that is a decision to respect, not an obstacle to route around. The value of the revised hypothesis is partly that it makes the true ceiling measurable instead of assumed.

The falsifiable version, and it is cheap to run: probe the eight candidate systems above. If enterprise reachability does not clear roughly 40%, the ceiling is real, the addressable market for this method is venture-backed technology companies, and the honest conclusion is that RoleFeed is a well-built tool pointed at the wrong segment. That would be worth knowing, and worth knowing for a day's work rather than a month's.

The structural insight

No applicant tracking system offers search. Every endpoint is scoped to one employer, so there is no query to write and nothing to crawl — you can only ask a named employer what it currently has open.

This inverts where the value sits. The code is commodity; the curated employer list is the product, and coverage is bounded precisely by who is on it. It also sets the ceiling: an employer running SuccessFactors, Taleo or iCIMS cannot be reached at all, which is most of the enterprise segment.

What it does

Every two hours a scheduled job asks each resolved employer for its current vacancies, applies the matching rules, merges the result with the previous run so a posting's first-seen date survives, and commits a single JSON snapshot. That commit rebuilds a static page. The page is the interface: filter by size, sector, employer and seniority; isolate roles under 24 hours old; shortlist or dismiss; and see how stale the data is and when the next collection is due.

Results are segmented by employer size rather than presented as one list, because a director title means something different at a 40-person scaleup than at Maersk, and the two are not usefully ranked against each other.

What it deliberately is not

  • Not a scraper. Every source is a documented or openly published endpoint. Where there is no legitimate route — British Airways publishes no feed and disallows its search path in robots.txt — the employer is recorded as uncovered rather than worked around.
  • Not a job board. One user. No accounts, no employers, no marketplace. Nothing is optimised for anyone but its owner.
  • Not an auto-applier. It surfaces; the applying is manual and stays manual. Volume is not the constraint being solved.
  • Not a framework project. One hand-written static file, no build step, no bundler. It deploys as static assets, costs nothing to run, and remains readable by its non-engineer author — which is a requirement, not a compromise.

Success criteria

  • Surfaces roles worth applying for that would not otherwise have been seen. Five was the threshold for proving the concept.
  • A new posting at a covered employer appears within two hours of publication.
  • Running cost stays at zero.
  • Its owner can explain every component of it, and change any of them.

Of those four, three are met. The first — the one the whole thing exists for — is not yet.

Roadmap

Under consideration, in dependency order
  • 1. Authentication. Access is currently URL-only, which is obscurity rather than security — and it unblocks everything below it, because both remaining items need state tied to an identity rather than to one browser. Cloudflare Access with a one-time e-mail code is free at this scale and needs no code.
  • 2. Scoring a role up or down, feeding back into the search. The interesting one, and the one with a catch worth stating: since no endpoint offers search, a signal cannot change a query — there is no query. It can only change two things, and both are worth doing. It can widen the employer list, by treating a thumbs-up as evidence about a sector and size band and proposing adjacent employers to add. And it can tune the title rules, by surfacing which patterns produce accepted roles and which produce noise, so the thresholds stop being guesses. The honest constraint is volume: a few dozen judgements will not train anything, but they will point a human at the right edits, which at this scale is more valuable than a model.
  • 3. Application tracking. Recording whether a role was applied for, and when. Cheap once state is server-side, and it closes the loop: it turns the feed into a record of the search itself, which is also what makes the 48-hour thesis testable rather than assumed.

Items 2 and 3 both require durable, shared state, which the current design has no place for: shortlists live in one browser's local storage and do not reach a phone. Cloudflare Workers KV behind a Pages Function is the intended answer — still free, still no database to run.

A note on sequencing, against the order above. Item 2 learns from judgements about the roles it shows, and the evidence says that sample is both small and skewed: 89% of the mid-sized and smaller employers reached are on venture-tech systems, against 31% of the enterprise ones. A feedback loop trained on that would optimise confidently towards American technology startups. The higher-value work is upstream and cheaper: probe the remaining candidate applicant tracking systems, weighted to the UK and enterprise market, then configure the Workday employers, then decide on structured data for whatever is left. Fix the input before building a feedback loop on top of it — a scoring signal over a biased sample does not correct the bias, it learns it.

Known limits

  • Enterprise coverage is structurally capped. Large employers run SuccessFactors, Taleo or iCIMS, none of which expose a usable endpoint. Workday is the one enterprise system covered, and each employer must be hand-configured.
  • Publication dates are only as good as the source. Four of the six adapters return a genuine date. Greenhouse returns a last-updated timestamp that moves whenever an employer fixes a typo, so those roles fall back to when RoleFeed first saw them — which is honest, but not the same thing.
  • Undocumented endpoints break without notice. Workday especially. A sudden drop to zero roles is treated as suspected breakage rather than an empty board.
  • Token discovery can mis-attribute. A guessed board token can resolve to an unrelated company. Short first-word tokens are refused and non-obvious matches are flagged for verification.
